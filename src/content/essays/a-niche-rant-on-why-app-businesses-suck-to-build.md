Iʼve spent a lot of my life thinking up different businesses i could build. Chances are, you have to.

The things you naturally look at are:

-   Can i build a competitive product here?
-   Who is the audience and can i actually sell to them?
-   How big is the addressable market?
-   Am I interested in this space?

And down we go through the list of questions before we make our mind up about starting vs not starting.

But I realised just last week that Iʼve always been missing a question on the list.

Perhaps one of the top 3 most important questions to ask is missing and I only realised what it was last week, 4 years into running my current startup…

Here it is:

> ‘How much permission are you going to have to ask for to build this business & will you get it?ʼ

Lemme break this down:

Some businesses you start, you can ideate, design, build, sell and grow essentially all on your own without having to ask somebody else to open a door for you.

A great example is an agency where you can find clients yourself and deliver the services yourself. The business grows as and when you want, not based on whether someone else allows you to move forward.

agencies require a lot of work and typically donʼt have crazy multiples, but the one thing they do give you is the sense the you can be in charge of your destiny and grow/succeed off of your own back.

Not all businesses are like this. Letʼs look at mobile apps:

From an outsiders perspective, mobile apps donʼt look like a big, permission seeking business. If you can build the app, list it on the app store and then find a way to drive people to it for free, profit!

The reality doesnʼt work like that at all, and nobody outside of the mobile app industry sees this:

Mobile apps in 2026 are so bloody competitive, with such fickle user bases.

Basically every app idea has been beaten to death at this point. If you miraculously think of an app that hasnʼt been built yet and has a viable audience, the second you ship it and it SNIFFS success, eleventeen chinese apps are going to be built to compete with you with infinite budgets, world class dev and aggressive IP theft.

And all of this competition is for extremely fickle userbases that are incredibly hard to engage even if you dont have a paywall.

And itʼs not their fault, nobody is excited by apps anymore and there are so many to choose from in every category, so of course people are not going to be sticky anymore

Let me show you what VC-backable app retention looks like:

Day 0 (install day): 100% of users

Day 1: 35% of users (65% of people leave on day fricking zero!!)

Day 7: 15% of users

Day 30: 7-10% of users

Within a month, 90%+ of your signups will have left your app never to return… AND THATʼS CONSIDERED GREAT!

And donʼt even get me started on conversion rates. They are so poor + subscriber retention month over month is so poor and price levels are so low, that itʼs near impossible to build a strong app business organically. You just canʼt drive enough people to your app to pay £6 one time and then churn.

So what do apps do instead?

They pile ‘small country GDPʼ size money into paid ads on facebook, instagram, tiktok etc.

Thats how almost every single mobile app grows - they build an app, and throw boatloads of cash into advertising (most of it unprofitable or requires over 12-18 months to recoup the inital ad spend), and then just hope that they figure out how to make the numbers work at scale so that they can sell it to someone else.

But hang on, you said app companies really struggle to make money from users, so where do they get all the money for the growth?

Hereʼs where the permission-seeking comes in:

The vast majority of app companies that run apps at scale, raise VC money. In exchange for that VC money, the founders essentially sign away control over their companies - They cannot sell their company when they wish, issue dividends, grant share options, build certain things, and a whole laundry list of other stuff.

That is all now controlled by ‘investor majority consentʼ. Which basically means anything omportant you want to do, you have to go and ask mummy and daddy VCs to say yes or no and you have to listen to them.

It doesnʼt even matter if they hold a small minority stake in your company! when you raise the money, they will force you to create a new class of preferred shares and those are the shares they buy. Then they get you to agree to all big decisions requiring majority vote of holders of those shares and those shares only, not all shares in the company.

Congratulations, if you raise VC money, your company is not actually yours anymore - itʼs somebody else that decides what can and cant be done whilst you just show up and do the hard work all day every day indefinitely.

This is where the permission seeking stops for a lot of businesses, but because the app business is so brutal, it goes further.

Because app users are so easy to churn, hardly convert and will not pay much money at all for any lengthy period of time, just using venture raised money is not enough to grow. You now need something called ‘user acquisition financeʼ.

Essentially a debt facility to get even more money to pile into growth, hoping that if you an spend money at scale with some semblance of interesting numbers, youʼll figure out the business model and profitability later. Or youʼll sell it onto someone else who inherits the problem of figuring out profitablility.

these UA finance providers will have their own permission terms they require you to sign + they will sign you up to a repayment plan and benchmarks you need to meet, else they will heavily penalise you.

congratulations, now you cant move the product much at all, because youʼre paying back obscene sums of money to debt providers that are expecting a certain level of performance that you cannot disrupt.

Mobile game studios have one other option, which is partnering with a publisher - you build the game and then they sell it and scale it for you and you work out some kind of revenue deal. Does that sound good? Well in practice, mobile game studios get taken to the absolute cleaners by publishers. 2 minutes of google searching will show you all the horror stories you need to know there. Not a good path either…

As you can see, building a business where yousell permissions to outside partners is a brutal path. As the founder, you are not running off your profits, you are onboarding more cash than you can handle putting your business is a lot of risk, and your destiny is controlled by somebody else whoʼs incentives arenʼt aligned with yours. you donʼt decide when you sell or how much you sell for, your VC masters do.

If someone comes to you with an acquisition ofer for letʼs say £30m, which would give you life changing money + 3x your VCs money in the space of 1.5 years, theyʼll likely say no and block the deal. Youʼre one of their potential winners, so they want you to work for years longer trying to get a billion dollar outcome (and potentially have the business die in the process).

How this materialises for you as a founder is essentially, you have to put all other aspects of your life on hold for the sake of the business.

The crazy thing is that VCs actually really like this. It gives them even more control.

You the founder are always running on finite runway as youʼre not profitable, so you canʼt really make any long term plans in your life. you canʼt really buy a house with your wife, or plan for the future, or say confidently where youʼll be in the next 1,2,3 years.

It all depends on whether you can raise more money, which VCs love because it means youʼll fight to the death to raise more cash at a higher valuation and then they can show paper returns to their investors (btw they can also block you from raising at certain valuations if they donʼt like it enough).

So why do so many founders run these extreme permission seeking businesses?

Well, a lot of people donʼt realise the nature of how these businesses work before they start, so they get naively caught up in it.

Also there are some thrill-seeking founders that look at the fact that their life will be owned and brutal and will require 6 years of all greenlights to make money, but there could be a big pot of money at the end if all goes well… and that excites them. These are usually the people that say ‘broʼ and ‘portfolioʼ in the same sentence often. A lot of them have rich parents anyway so theyʼll be absolutely fine.

So what is the point iʼm trying to make?

If youʼre thinking about starting a business, It could be helpful to ask yourself the question:

> ‘Is there anyone whoʼs permission I would need to be successful here Or can I build a company off of the hard work and profits the me/my cofounders and I can generate?ʼ

If your goal is to become a billionaire - yeah, your business options likely all require permission from somebody. But i hope you realise that setting out to build a business to become a billionaire is the same as wanting a job and going to ask the Real Madrid if theyʼll hire you to replace mbappe.

Possible if you have the insane luck, but unlikely and could be a big waste of time.

If you want to be wealthy, take care of your family, have nice things and go on nice holidays, but donʼt care about the ego symbol of becoming a billionaire. It could be worth looking at building a business that doesnʼt require external permission to win.

at least that way, you can be in charge of your own destiny, build at your own pace and take a dividend/get acquired when you hear a number that works for you.

I chose the permission path at 22 years old when I raised $$$ millions of VC money for my startup Carter.

I regret nothing, but iʼm 26 today and looking at building other stuff. I deffo wouldnʼt do the VC path again. Iʼm much more likely to hit my goal financial outcome off my own back without someone else sat on my shoulder controlling my outcomes.

Something to think about.
