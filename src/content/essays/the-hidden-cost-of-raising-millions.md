I’ve spent a lot of my life thinking up different businesses ideas & companies I could build. Chances are if you’re reading this, you have to.

You come up with a business idea and the questions you naturally start asking yourself ask are:

-   Can I build a competitive product here?
    
-   Who is the customer and can I actually sell to them?
    
-   How big is the addressable market?
    
-   Am I actually interested in this space?
    

And down we go through a list of questions before we make our mind up about starting that particular business, or not starting it.

But I realise now after building my startup for the last 4 years, raising millions of VC dollars, hiring a team etc…

That I’ve always missed one question off the list.

Perhaps the most important question of them all:

*‘How much permission am I going to have to ask from other people to make this business a success & will I actually get that permission?’*

Lemme explain:

For most businesses you start, you can ideate, design, build, and sell essentially all by yourself and/or with a small team.

This is what I would call a ‘permission-less business’: Essentially a buisness that doesn’t require a third party, outside of the business to open a door for you.

You can open all doors yourself.

A great example of a ‘permission-less business’ is a service-based business, where you can both find clients, deliver the services and grow the business all by yourself. The amount that you grow is directly correlated with how much effort you put in and there isn’t really any other meaningful factor that determines the business’ success.

Not all businesses have this perfect correlation between effort and success. Yes it’s always true that the more effort you put in, the higher your PROBABILITY of success is. But there are some businesses where effort alone just isn’t enough - You also need third party resources and crucially, their permission to win.

This is what I call (you guessed it) a ‘permission-based’ business.

The example i’ll walk through for a ‘permission-based business’ is a mobile app studio - That’s the kind of company I built.

From an outsiders perspective, mobile apps don’t look like a business that needs anyone’s permission; An outsider would think:

“If you can build the app, list it on the app store and then find a way to drive people to it for less than they pay)… Profit!”

What a lovely idea… The reality doesn’t work like that at all, and nobody outside of the mobile app industry looking in actually sees this:

In 2025, 50,000 new apps got published every single day…

Which means we are long past the phase of getting excited about any new app (everybody is too busy on TikTok or Instagram)…

Which means getting users to a new app is incredibly expensive with paid ads, or incredibly unreliable with organic growth…

Which means a minuscule fraction of users you acquire actually buy your subscription and continue to pay after the first month…

Which inevitably means you as the founder either have to be happy being tiny and just relying on free organic growth (which we got over a million users purely organically before spending a penny on ads, still incredibly tough to make real money), **or you accept being a loss-making company indefinitely and instead chase aggressive growth with external capital from investors & debt providers.**

That’s where the ‘permission’ part comes in.

Every app installed on your phone right now has raised tens, to hundreds of millions of dollars. The ones you actually still use on a semi-regular basis have raised billions of dollars.

They raise this money to throw into advertising for the sole purpose of growing user numbers, irrespective of whether it’s profitable growth or not.

Why do they do this? How does that make any sense?

Well, mobile app businesses suck in terms of making profit, but they’re great for getting massive user numbers if they can pay for the growth, which makes them weirdly actually great businesses for *some* investors.

Apps can usually justify higher and higher valuations (on paper) based on growth rate, and there always seems to be institutional buyers at the end of the chain, to acquire fat app companies. The buyer has the bankroll to absorb the losses and gets some revenue or valuation benefit from app’s growth numbers.

The system kinda works for everyone…

Except for you, the founder of the app company.

Because when you raise money and start playing the valuation-hopping game, you lose control over the company you started.

You have to sign agreements saying that for every big decision, whether it’s deciding to sell the company, issue dividends, build certain products, everything now requires you to get permission from the firms that gave you the growth capital.

The permission part makes total logical sense! If your building a business where the value is not in its profitability, but rather in it’s aggressive growth and you need other people’s money to fund that, It would be stupid for them to not have control over what you can and can’t do as the founder.

Investors aren’t evil for taking control away from the founder, they’re doing the exact right thing for THEIR business, which is to deliver returns to their customers: the LPs who’s money their investing.

You just have to realise that for you as a founder when you raise money, the deal structure required to onboard that capital means that your company isn’t really yours anymore - Yeah you run the day to day, but the big decisions and importantly, the end outcome are now directed by other people.

Often times it’s not just one group of people, it’s lots of different groups of people, all with different incentives and success metrics:

-   You seed investor that invested at a $10m valuation will be happy for you to sell the company for $100m. They 10x’d their money in a few years!
    
-   Your A-round investor that gave you money at a $50m valuation… not so happy.
    

The app business especially has so many different groups that control your outcome - Most big app studios run out of the amount of VC money that they can raise, so they also start raising a tonne of debt money in the form of something called ‘user acquisition finance’.

Essentially this is a debt finance facility to onboard EVEN MORE money to pile into paid ads and user growth.

The entire thing you’re betting on as the founder is: If you can spend all this VC and debt money at scale, with some semblance of interesting KPIs, you’ll figure out the business model at some scale and all numbers will suddenly turn green… Or like I said earlier, you’ll sell the company to someone else who can just tuck away the the losses on their own balance sheet.

When you take on this UA finance debt stuff, you’ll have a new list of permissions you need to agree to, but also now you have a debt repayment plan and benchmarks you need to meet to avoid getting penalised, or at the very worst, defaulting on the debt and being declared insolvent.

Now as the founder, it’s not just the big decisions that you can’t make on your own. It’s also day-to-day decisions on things like app updates and pricing tiers.

You have to pay back massive sums of debt money at an expected level of return, that you cannot afford to take any risk with an experimental product update.

This really kills the founder enthusiasm that made you start the business in the first place…

\_

As you can probably tell by now, building a ‘growth-at-all-costs’ permission-based business where you trade control in exchange for growth money, is a brutal path.

You are not running your businesses off of its profits, you are instead onboarding more cash from third parties than you can handle which puts your business in a lot of risk, and your destiny in the hands of others who’s incentives aren’t completely aligned with yours.

Practically how this materialises for you as the founder is:

-   You have to put all other aspects of your life on hold because of the uncertainty of the business.
    
-   You are always running on finite runway. You’re not profitable, so you can’t really make any long term plans in your life - You can’t really buy a house with your wife in case you can’t pay the mortgage one day. You can’t really plan for the future because it depends so heavily on what will happen with the business.
    
-   You can’t actually communicate where you’ll be in the next 1 - 3 years. This makes for very difficult conversations with your loved ones, especially if you have somebody in your life that’s looking to build a future with you.
    

Everything depends on whether you can consistently raise more money at more and more impressive valuations to keep the charade active without any profit.

*So why do so many founders run these extreme permission-based businesses?*

Well, you have a few different camps of founders that do this:

1.  Some founders are 100% mission-driven with a massive ambitious idea that requires an enormous amount of capital. These folks seemingly don’t care much about personal outcomes, they just want the opportunity and resources to build their vision. These are the best founders that truly do push humanity forward.
    

But unfortunately, these aren’t the majority of founders that get all this external capital. They’re actually a very small minority.

2.  Most founders that build permission-based companies are in effect thrill-seekers that DO understand that their life will be uncertain for a decade and will require ‘all green lights’ to see any personal financial outcomes, but there could be a big pot of money at the end if all goes well… and that lottery ticket excites them.
    

These are usually the founders that often say ‘bro’ and ‘portfolio’ in the same sentence and they also usually have large safety nets through family, so the downside isn’t really a big deal

3.  The final bucket are founders that dont actually realise how these ‘growth-at-all-odds’ permission businesses actually work before they onboard the capital. They are usually young, talented kids that see the glitz and glam of other founders bragging about massive valuations and big funding rounds.
    

When the opportunity comes around for them to do the same thing, they of course jump on and grab with both hands. I’d say this is the category I fell into - I had just turned 23 when I raised money.

So what is the point i’m trying to make?

If you’re thinking about starting a business, It could be helpful to ask yourself the question:

*‘do I need anybody else’s permission to make this business successful, or can I grow the business from just the hard work and profits that me and my cofounders can generate?’*

If your goal is to become a billionaire - yeah, your business ideas likely all require permission from somebody.

But i hope you realise that building a business to become a billionaire, is the same as wanting a job and going to ask the head coach at Real Madrid if they’ll hire you to replace Mbappe.

Definitely possible that it works, at the end of the day that’s how all the self made entrepreneurs on the Forbes list made their money.

But I personally don’t like the odds.

9 times out of 10, you waste a lot of time with little to nothing at the end.

On the other hand, if your goal is to be wealthy enough to be independent, take care of your family, have nice things and go on nice holidays, but you don’t care about the ego symbol of becoming a billionaire, It could be worth looking at building a business that doesn’t require so much external permission to win.

At least that way you can be in charge of your own destiny, build something sustainable and take dividends or get acquired when you hear a number that works for you.

I chose the permission-based path at 23 years old. 4 years later, we shut the company down. The company didn’t work out, which is all my own fault entirely. Nobody is to blame for a company failing other than the founders.

The journey of building that business, raising the money and trying to pull of something really big, taught me more than anything else ever has and that’s something I am eternally thankful for.

But i’m 26 years old today and one of the biggest lessons I learnt from that journey, is that I’m much more likely to make all the money I need for myself and the people close to me, off my own back without needing anybody else’s permission.

Something to ponder.
